Most people over 60 have no idea whether their strength is normal, declining, or genuinely strong for their age, because nobody ever gives them a number to measure against. The 30-second chair stand test fixes that. It's the single most useful at-home strength check I know of: it takes half a minute, needs only a sturdy chair, and it predicts something that actually matters, your risk of falling.

Here's how to do it, then how you stack up.

**The test:** Sit in a sturdy chair (seat about 17 inches high), arms crossed over your chest so you can't push off. On "go," stand all the way up and sit all the way down as many times as you can in 30 seconds. Count each full stand. Stop if you feel pain or dizziness.

## What your score means

If you land **inside the range** for your age, your lower-body strength is typical. **Above the range** is genuinely above average, a good sign your legs and balance are holding up well. **Below the range** is the one to pay attention to: research using this test links below-average scores to a higher risk of falls, which is the single biggest threat to independence as we age.

A below-average score is not a verdict, it's a starting point. Lower-body strength is one of the most trainable things in the body at any age. Adults in their 80s and 90s routinely add several stands to their score within a couple of months of consistent practice.

## How to improve your number

The fastest way to get better at standing up is, unsurprisingly, to practice standing up. Sit-to-stands done as an exercise, two or three sets of 8 to 12, a few days a week, build exactly the muscles the test measures. Add a bit of support (a hand on the counter) if you need it at first, then remove it as you get stronger. Progress shows up fast, which is why this is such a motivating place to start.

*This is general information, not medical or fitness advice. Talk with your doctor or a physical therapist before starting a new exercise program, especially if you have heart, joint, or balance concerns.*
