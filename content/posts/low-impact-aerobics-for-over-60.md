The single most important thing most adults over 60 can do for their long-term health isn't [strength training](/how-much-strength-training-do-seniors-actually-need-to-live-longer/), isn't stretching, and it's not any particular diet. It's consistent aerobic activity, and low-impact formats are the most sustainable way to get it. The CDC recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week for older adults, and low-impact exercise is fully capable of meeting that threshold.

That matters because "low-impact" often gets misread as "easy" or "not quite real exercise." Neither is true. Low-impact simply means you keep at least one foot on the ground at all times, which protects joints while still delivering genuine cardiovascular stress. Your heart rate goes up. Calories burn. Bone-loading still occurs (more on that below). The workout is real.

## What "Moderate Intensity" Actually Means for You

The classic intensity test is called the Talk Test, and it's one of the most practical tools the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) endorses. At moderate intensity, you can answer a question in a short sentence but you'd struggle to deliver a monologue. If you can recite a paragraph of poetry without pausing, you're probably not working hard enough. If you genuinely can't get three words out, back off.

A heart rate monitor adds precision. The commonly used formula for estimated maximum heart rate is 220 minus your age, though the ACSM notes this carries real individual variation. For a 68-year-old, that gives an estimated max around 152 beats per minute, and moderate intensity sits roughly between 64% and 76% of that, so somewhere in the 97-115 bpm range. Those aren't hard rules, they're a starting framework. If you're on beta-blockers or other cardiac medications, heart rate targets lose most of their meaning and you'll want guidance from your physician before using them.

## The Formats That Actually Work (and One That Gets Overhyped)

**Walking** remains what the National Institute on Aging (NIA) calls the most accessible form of physical activity for older adults, and there's nothing reductive about that. A brisk 30-minute walk, done five days a week, satisfies the CDC recommendation on its own. The key word is brisk. A slow stroll through the grocery store doesn't count, however many steps it generates. You need pace that elevates your breathing.

**Water aerobics and pool walking** are worth highlighting separately because they're often treated as "beginner" activities or recovery-only options. That framing is wrong. Water provides resistance in all directions, which means your muscles work harder than land-based movement at the same speed. The buoyancy reduces joint compression dramatically, making aquatic exercise genuinely suitable for people with significant osteoarthritis or recent joint replacement. The Arthritis Foundation explicitly recommends aquatic exercise programs, and many hospital systems and YMCAs run structured water aerobics classes specifically designed for older adults.

**Stationary and recumbent cycling** give you cardiovascular work without any balance demands. Recumbent bikes in particular are valuable if you have lower back issues or limited hip mobility, since the reclined seat takes compression off the lumbar spine. A recumbent bike also removes the fall risk that upright cycling carries for anyone with balance concerns.

**Dance-based classes** including Zumba Gold, ballroom dance, and line dancing have something the other formats lack: they're genuinely fun for a large number of people, which matters enormously for adherence. They also train reactive balance passively, since you're constantly shifting weight and changing direction. The NIA notes that dance-style activities improve both aerobic fitness and coordination simultaneously.

The format that tends to get overhyped for this population is **elliptical training.** It's not harmful, but it demands more balance than most people assume, particularly during the transition from moving forward to stepping off the machine. For anyone with balance concerns, a stationary bike is a safer choice with similar cardiovascular benefit.

## The Bone Density Question

One legitimate concern about low-impact exercise: it doesn't load the skeleton the way running or jumping does. For people managing osteopenia or osteoporosis, that matters. [Water aerobics](/water-aerobics-exercises-for-seniors/), while excellent for joints, provides almost no bone-loading stimulus. Cycling, same problem.

This doesn't mean you should avoid these activities. It means you probably shouldn't rely on them exclusively if bone density is a priority. Brisk walking does provide some mechanical load to the hips and spine. The NIA recommends combining aerobic activity with weight-bearing movement and [resistance training](/what-the-new-acsm-resistance-training-guidelines-mean-after-60/) for bone health, not picking one to the exclusion of the others.

If bone density is a clinical concern, have that conversation with your physician or a physical therapist before designing your program.

## How to Build the Habit Without Injuring Yourself in Week One

The most common mistake people make when starting or restarting aerobic exercise isn't going too slow. It's accumulating too much volume too fast. The ACSM recommends increasing exercise duration by no more than about 10% per week to manage injury risk, though that's a rough guideline, not a law of physics.

A practical starting structure for someone who hasn't been exercising regularly:

1. Start with three sessions per week, 15-20 minutes each.
2. At week three, add a fourth session rather than extending time.
3. At week five or six, begin extending sessions toward 25-30 minutes.
4. Build to five sessions of 30 minutes before worrying about intensity.
5. Only after you're consistent at 150 minutes per week should you experiment with adding higher-intensity intervals.

The step where people most often go wrong is step three. Adding a fourth session feels harder than it looks on paper, and that's often the point where soreness convinces people they've done damage. Muscle fatigue from new activity is normal. Joint pain, significant swelling, or pain that persists more than 48 hours after a session warrants a check with your doctor or a physical therapist before continuing.
