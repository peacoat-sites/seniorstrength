Most men I work with who are 70 don't think of themselves as "seniors." They think of themselves as guys who used to run 5Ks, or who built a deck with their own hands twenty years ago, or who want to keep up with grandkids who are now somehow in high school. That gap between who they feel they are and what their body will currently let them do, that's usually what brings them to my office.

So let's skip the gentle disclaimers and talk about what actually works.

---

## What Your Body Actually Needs at 70 (It's Not What Most Articles Say)

Here's where I'll push back on a lot of generic advice: the fitness world is obsessed with cardio for older adults. Walk more. Take the stairs. Get your heart rate up. And yes, cardiovascular health matters. But in 25 years of working with people in this age group, the men who lose independence, who fall, who can't get up off the floor, who stop driving, who can't lift their own luggage, they almost always lost it because of muscle, not heart rate.

Sarcopenia, the gradual loss of muscle mass that accelerates after 60, is the real threat. After 70, most men are losing somewhere between 1 and 2 percent of their muscle mass per year without deliberate resistance training. That's not a scare tactic. That's just biology being honest with you.

The good news? Muscle responds to training at 70. It responds more slowly than it did at 35, and recovery takes longer, but it absolutely responds. A 2021 meta-analysis in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* looked at resistance training in adults over 65 and found meaningful gains in strength and physical function across the board. You're not fighting a losing battle. You're just fighting a different one than you did at 40.

A well-designed routine for a 70-year-old man has four priorities, roughly in this order: strength, balance, mobility, and cardiovascular endurance. Most programs have that backwards, or skip two of those entirely.

## The Actual Routine: What to Do and When

Before I lay this out, one honest caveat: this is a starting framework, not a prescription. If you have osteoporosis, a history of spinal compression fractures, joint replacements, or uncontrolled blood pressure, please clear this with your physician or a physical therapist before adding resistance training. I'm serious about that, not as a legal hedge, but because those conditions genuinely change what's appropriate.

That said, here's what I'd build for a reasonably healthy 70-year-old man who hasn't been consistently exercising.

**Weeks 1 through 4: Build the foundation**

Start with three days of strength training per week, never back to back. Monday, Wednesday, Friday works well. Each session should be 30 to 40 minutes total.

The exercises that give you the most return at this stage are the ones that mimic how you actually move in life:

- **Sit-to-stand from a chair** (essentially a bodyweight squat). Start with a regular dining chair. Do 2 sets of 10. This single exercise predicts functional independence better than almost any other measure.
- **Goblet squat with a light dumbbell** (5 to 10 lbs) once the bodyweight version feels easy.
- **Wall push-ups**, progressing to incline push-ups on a counter, then a low table.
- **Resistance band rows** anchored to a door. 2 sets of 12.
- **Glute bridges** lying on your back. This one is non-negotiable because the glutes are your body's primary fall-prevention muscle and most men over 70 have barely-functional glutes.
- **Standing calf raises** holding a countertop. Simple, deeply underrated.

On the days between strength sessions, do something low-intensity for 20 to 30 minutes. A walk around the neighborhood counts. So does light cycling on a stationary bike or swimming. The goal here isn't fitness, exactly. It's keeping the body moving and aiding recovery.

**After the first month**

Progress isn't about lifting heavier for the sake of it. It's about doing the same movements with more control, more range of motion, and eventually more load. When 2 sets of 10 feels easy and your form is solid, go to 3 sets. When 3 sets feels controlled and you could keep going, add 5 lbs or move to a heavier resistance band.

A reasonable target for many men at this stage: within 4 to 6 months, working toward dumbbell goblet squats with 15 to 25 lbs, dumbbell rows at 15 to 20 lbs, and proper push-ups from the floor. Not everyone will get there on that timeline, and that's fine.

## Balance Training Is Where Falls Get Prevented

I'll be direct: most men skip balance work because it doesn't feel like "real" exercise. It can look a little silly. You're standing on one leg in your living room, not lifting anything impressive.

Do it anyway.

Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death in adults over 65, according to the CDC. One in four Americans over 65 falls each year. A broken hip in a 70-year-old man carries a genuinely sobering 1-year mortality rate of around 20 to 30 percent. These aren't statistics to alarm you. They're reasons to treat balance work as seriously as anything else in your routine.

The progression I use with clients:

1. Stand near a countertop and practice single-leg stance for 10 to 30 seconds each side. Touch the counter if you need to.
2. Progress to doing this with eyes closed. Harder than it sounds.
3. Add movement: standing on one leg while slowly moving the opposite leg forward and back, like a slow pendulum.
4. Try tandem standing, one foot directly in front of the other, heel to toe. Hold for 30 seconds.
5. Eventually, try slow single-leg stance on a folded towel or a balance pad (the Airex Balance Pad, around $40, is widely used in PT clinics and worth owning).

Ten minutes, three times a week, incorporated at the end of your strength sessions. That's the whole ask.

## The Mobility Piece Most People Skip Until They Regret It

Tight hips and a stiff thoracic spine, which means the middle of your back, are the two things that silently make everything harder. They limit your squat depth, your walking gait, your ability to look over your shoulder while driving, and your posture. They often don't hurt until they do.

You don't need a 45-minute yoga class. Here's what I actually give most of my clients:

- **Hip flexor stretch** (a half-kneeling lunge position, held 45 to 60 seconds per side, done daily if possible)
- **Cat-cow on hands and knees**, 10 slow repetitions, which is one of the best ways to maintain spinal mobility and costs nothing
- **Thoracic rotation in a chair**: sit tall, arms across your chest, slowly rotate left and right as far as you comfortably can, 10 reps each direction
- **90/90 hip stretch** on the floor if you can get down there comfortably

Spend 10 minutes on this daily. Morning works well, after a warm shower when the tissues are a little looser. This isn't about flexibility in the gymnastics sense. It's about keeping the range of motion you need to move safely and without compensation.

## A Word About Protein (Because the Routine Won't Work Without It)

The training is the stimulus. Protein is the raw material. Most men over 70 are eating nowhere near enough protein to support muscle synthesis, and current research suggests the RDA of 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight is almost certainly too low for older adults engaged in resistance training.

A reasonable target: closer to 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 175-pound man, that's roughly 95 to 125 grams of protein daily. If you're not tracking, you're probably getting around 60 to 70 grams on a good day.

Practical sources that aren't complicated: eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, canned salmon or tuna, chicken, lean beef, edamame. Protein shakes aren't magic, but a simple whey or pea protein shake can make hitting the target realistic without forcing you to eat all day. Optimum Nutrition's Gold Standard Whey runs about $35 to $40 for a 2-pound tub and is about as straightforward as it gets.

You might be wondering if supplements beyond protein are worth it. Creatine monohydrate, honestly, is the one I'd mention. The evidence for creatine in older adults is genuinely good, including a 2017 review in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* that found consistent support for maintaining muscle mass and function in older populations. Five grams a day. That's it. Cheap, safe, well-studied.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many days a week should a 70-year-old man work out?

Three to four days of intentional exercise per week is a solid target: two to three days of strength training plus at least two days of walking, swimming, or light cardio. Rest and recovery genuinely matter more at 70 than they did at 40, so building in non-training days isn't laziness, it's part of the plan.

### Is it safe for a 70-year-old man to lift heavy weights?

Generally yes, with proper technique and a sensible progression. "Heavy" is relative. The goal isn't max-effort powerlifting; it's working hard enough to create a stimulus for muscle adaptation. Most men should get a baseline medical clearance and ideally a few sessions with a qualified trainer or physical therapist before significantly increasing loads.

### What's the single best exercise for a 70-year-old man?

If I had to pick one, it's the squat pattern, specifically sit-to-stand from a chair. It builds lower body strength, tests and improves balance, mimics a movement you do dozens of times a day, and its performance correlates strongly with long-term functional independence. Practice it daily.

### How long before a 70-year-old man sees results from exercise?

Most people notice improved energy and daily movement quality within 3 to 4 weeks. Measurable strength gains typically begin around 6 to 8 weeks. Visible muscle changes take longer, often 3 to 6 months. The less conditioned you are starting out, the faster the initial progress tends to be.

### Should a 70-year-old man do cardio or strength training first?

If you're doing both in the same session, do strength first. Cardiovascular fatigue will compromise your form and effort on resistance exercises. If they're on separate days, the order is less important. Most men this age do better keeping cardio and strength sessions separate when possible.

---

None of this has to happen all at once. The men I've seen make the best progress are the ones who start with two things, do them consistently, and add from there. Not the ones who launched a full program on January 2nd and injured themselves by February. Start smaller than you think you need to. The consistency will get you further than the intensity every single time.
