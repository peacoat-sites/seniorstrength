Most men I work with who are 70 don't think of themselves as "seniors." They think of themselves as guys who used to run 5Ks, or who built a deck with their own hands twenty years ago, or who want to keep up with grandkids who are now somehow in high school. That gap between who they feel they are and what their body will currently let them do, that's usually what brings them to my office.

So let's skip the gentle disclaimers and talk about what actually works.

## What Your Body Actually Needs at 70 (It's Not What Most Articles Say)

Here's where I'll push back on conventional wisdom: the fitness world is obsessed with cardio for older adults. Walk more. Take the stairs. Get your heart rate up. Yes, cardiovascular health matters. But in 25 years of working with people in this age group, the men who lose independence, who fall, who can't get up off the floor, who stop driving, who can't lift their own luggage, they lost it because of [muscle, not heart rate](/benefits-of-exercise-for-older-adults/).

Sarcopenia is the real culprit. That's the gradual loss of muscle mass that accelerates after 60. After 70, most men are losing somewhere between 1 and 2 percent of their muscle mass per year without deliberate [resistance training](/how-to-start-a-senior-fitness-program/). Not a scare tactic. Just biology.

The bright side? Muscle responds at 70. Slower than it did at 35, with longer recovery, but it responds. A 2021 meta-analysis in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* looked at resistance training in adults over 65 and found meaningful gains in strength and physical function. You're not losing. You're just playing by different rules than you were at 40.

A solid routine for a 70-year-old man needs four priorities in this order: strength, balance, mobility, and cardiovascular endurance. Most programs have it backwards or skip half of them entirely.

## The Actual Routine: What to Do and When

One honest caveat before I lay this out: this is a starting framework, not a prescription. If you have osteoporosis, a history of spinal compression fractures, joint replacements, or uncontrolled blood pressure, clear this with your physician or a physical therapist before adding resistance training. I'm serious. Those conditions genuinely change what's appropriate.

For a reasonably healthy 70-year-old man who hasn't been consistently exercising, here's what I'd build.

**Weeks 1 through 4: Build the foundation**

Start with three days of strength training per week, never back to back. Monday, Wednesday, Friday works well. Thirty to 40 minutes total per session.

The exercises that pay off at this stage are the ones that match how you actually move:

- **Sit-to-stand from a chair** (essentially a bodyweight squat). Start with a regular dining chair. Do 2 sets of 10. This single exercise predicts [functional independence](/exercises-for-seniors-to-stay-independent/) better than almost any other measure.
- **Goblet squat with a light dumbbell** (5 to 10 lbs) once the bodyweight version feels easy.
- **Wall push-ups**, progressing to incline push-ups on a counter, then a low table.
- **Resistance band rows** anchored to a door. 2 sets of 12.
- **Glute bridges** lying on your back. Non-negotiable. The glutes are your body's primary fall-prevention muscle and most men over 70 have barely-functional ones.
- **Standing calf raises** holding a countertop. Simple. Deeply underrated.

On the days between strength sessions, do something low-intensity for 20 to 30 minutes. A walk around the neighborhood counts. Light cycling on a stationary bike works. Swimming works. The goal isn't fitness in the traditional sense. It's keeping the body moving and aiding recovery.

**After the first month**

Progress isn't about lifting heavier for its own sake. It's about doing the same movements with more control, more range of motion, and eventually more load. When 2 sets of 10 feels easy and your form is solid, go to 3 sets. When 3 sets feels controlled and you could keep going, add 5 lbs or move to a heavier resistance band.

A reasonable target within 4 to 6 months: dumbbell goblet squats at 15 to 25 lbs, dumbbell rows at 15 to 20 lbs, and proper push-ups from the floor. Not everyone hits that timeline, and that's fine.

## Balance Training Is Where Falls Get Prevented

Most men skip balance work because it doesn't feel like "real" exercise. Standing on one leg in your living room doesn't look impressive.

Do it anyway.

Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death in adults over 65, according to the CDC. One in four Americans over 65 falls each year. A broken hip at 70 carries a 1-year mortality rate around 20 to 30 percent. These aren't alarm tactics. They're reasons to take balance work as seriously as anything else.

The progression I use:

1. Stand near a countertop and practice single-leg stance for 10 to 30 seconds each side. Touch the counter if you need to.
2. Progress to eyes closed. Much harder.
3. Add movement: standing on one leg while slowly moving the opposite leg forward and back.
4. Try tandem standing, one foot directly in front of the other, heel to toe. Hold for 30 seconds.
5. Eventually, try single-leg stance on a folded towel or balance pad (the Airex Balance Pad runs around $40, widely used in PT clinics).

Ten minutes three times a week, tucked in at the end of your strength sessions. That's it.

## The Mobility Piece Most People Skip Until They Regret It

Tight hips and a stiff thoracic spine (the middle of your back) silently make everything harder. They limit squat depth, walking gait, your ability to look over your shoulder while driving, and posture. They often don't hurt until they do.

You don't need a 45-minute yoga class. Here's what I give most clients:

- **Hip flexor stretch** (a half-kneeling lunge position, held 45 to 60 seconds per side, done daily if possible)
- **Cat-cow on hands and knees**, 10 slow repetitions, one of the best ways to maintain spinal mobility and costs nothing
- **Thoracic rotation in a chair**: sit tall, arms across your chest, slowly rotate left and right as far as you comfortably can, 10 reps each direction
- **90/90 hip stretch** on the floor if you can get down there comfortably

Spend 10 minutes daily. Morning works well, after a warm shower when tissues are a little looser. This isn't about flexibility in the gymnastics sense. It's about keeping the range of motion you need to move safely.

The training is the stimulus. Protein is the raw material. Most men over 70 aren't eating nearly enough protein to support muscle synthesis, and current research suggests the RDA of 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight is too low for older adults doing resistance training.

A reasonable target: closer to 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 175-pound man, that's roughly 95 to 125 grams of protein daily. If you're not tracking, you're probably getting around 60 to 70 grams on a good day.

Practical sources that aren't complicated: eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, canned salmon or tuna, chicken, lean beef, edamame. Protein shakes aren't magic, but a simple whey or pea protein shake can make hitting the target realistic without forcing you to eat all day. Optimum Nutrition's Gold Standard Whey runs about $35 to $40 for a 2-pound tub and is straightforward.

You might wonder if supplements beyond protein matter. Creatine monohydrate is the one I'd mention. The evidence for creatine in older adults is genuinely solid, including a 2017 review in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* that found consistent support for maintaining muscle mass and function in older populations. Five grams a day. Cheap, safe, well-studied.

None of this has to happen all at once. The men who make the best progress are the ones who start with two things, do them consistently, and add from there. Not the ones who launched a full program on January 2nd and injured themselves by February. Start smaller than you think you need to. Consistency beats intensity every single time.
