Something surprising came out of the Endocrine Society's ENDO 2026 conference in Chicago this month, and I don't think it's getting nearly enough attention. Researchers tracked wearable fitness device data from 753 patients with obesity who had started a GLP-1 receptor agonist, drugs like Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Zepbound, and found that physical activity actually *decreased* significantly after they began treatment. Not slightly. Significantly. As [Healio reported on June 13](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20260613/exercise-declined-in-patients-with-obesity-after-starting-a-glp1), people moved less once they started the medication, not more. If you're over 60 and currently taking one of these drugs, that finding should stop you cold. Because for older adults specifically, the combination of GLP-1-driven muscle loss and reduced movement isn't just a missed opportunity. It's a genuine health risk.

## The Muscle Problem Nobody Is Talking About Loudly Enough

Here's what gets glossed over in all the excitement around GLP-1 drugs: semaglutide and tirzepatide don't just burn fat. They reduce lean muscle mass alongside it. For a 35-year-old, that's manageable. For someone in their 60s or 70s, it's different, because you're already losing muscle to begin with.

The process has a name: sarcopenia. It's the age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength that kicks in around your 40s for most people and accelerates hard after 60. We're talking about a loss of 1 to 2 percent of muscle mass per year, with strength declining even faster than mass. Add a GLP-1 drug into that, and you're potentially speeding up something that was already working against you.

A review published in PMC in June 2026 specifically examined the risk of what researchers are calling "sarcopenic obesity" in older adults on GLP-1 medications. The real concern isn't just what happens while you're taking the drug. It's the weight cycling that happens if you stop. The review warns that older adults who discontinue GLP-1 therapy regain fat at a disproportionate rate compared to the muscle they lost, which means your body composition can actually be worse than when you started. You weigh the same on the scale but you're carrying more fat and less functional muscle. That has consequences: higher fall risk, reduced mobility, and accelerated functional decline.

## Why the Activity Drop Is So Dangerous at This Stage

The ENDO 2026 activity data bothered me most when I first read it. The obvious assumption, and one I held for a while, is that people on GLP-1 drugs feel better as they lose weight and naturally move more. Less joint pain, more energy, more momentum. The wearable data from those 753 patients said the opposite happened.

There are explanations for this. GLP-1 drugs slow gastric emptying and reduce appetite dramatically, which means people eat far less. Eating less means less fuel for physical activity, and some patients report fatigue, nausea, and general low energy, especially early on. The body is in a significant caloric deficit. It conserves. Movement goes down.

For adults over 60, this is a problem. Movement, especially resistance-based movement, is one of the primary tools we have for preserving muscle. When activity drops at the same time the medication is reducing lean tissue, you're losing on two fronts. As the [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12391595/) frames it, the risk isn't theoretical. It's a documented physiological trajectory for older adults on these medications without a structured muscle-preservation strategy.

## What the Research Actually Recommends

| Metric | Standard RDA | GLP-1 Therapeutic Range | Example: 170 lb (77 kg) Person |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Protein Target | 0.8 g/kg body weight | 1.2-1.6 g/kg body weight | 62 g/day | 92-123 g/day |

The protein targets being discussed in clinical circles now are notably higher than what most people over 60 are eating. Current expert guidance from a June 2026 piece in Diabetes In Control recommends that older adults on GLP-1 therapy aim for 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily. The standard RDA sits at 0.8 grams per kilogram. That's significant. A 170-pound (77 kg) person at the standard RDA would target about 62 grams of protein a day. At the higher therapeutic range, that jumps to 92 to 123 grams.

When appetite is already suppressed by the medication, hitting those protein targets requires intentional planning. You can't just eat when you're hungry. That's a conversation worth having with your physician or a registered dietitian before you assume your reduced appetite is guiding you right.

On exercise, the recommendation isn't cardio. It's resistance training: two to three sessions per week targeting the major muscle groups. I want to be careful here because "resistance training for older adults" gets reduced to resistance bands and five-pound dumbbells in popular coverage, and the research doesn't actually support that level of intensity for muscle preservation. The stimulus needs to be meaningful. That doesn't mean dangerous or extreme, but it does mean progressive. Working with a trainer or physical therapist who specializes in older adults is worth considering, particularly when you're managing the fatigue and appetite changes that come with GLP-1 use.

## The Research Gap and What's Coming

The Stanford angle caught me. Researchers there published findings in June 2026 on a compound that may help GLP-1 users preserve muscle health during treatment, and the language they used was telling. They called it "a major unmet need." That phrase, from researchers who study this professionally, tells you where the field stands. The drugs have moved faster than the muscle-preservation protocols designed to go alongside them.

The ACSM's 2026 Worldwide Fitness Trends report, based on a survey of 2,000 exercise professionals, ranked fitness programs specifically designed for older adults at their highest-ever position since the report launched in 2006. The report explicitly cited the rise of GLP-1 medication use as a contributing factor. The fitness industry is responding to what clinicians and patients are experiencing. The infrastructure is building. But it hasn't caught up yet.

We don't have long-term randomized controlled trials on the optimal resistance training protocols for older adults specifically on GLP-1 therapy. What we have is solid mechanistic understanding, real clinical concern, and early data pointing clearly in one direction: if you're over 60 and taking one of these medications, doing nothing about muscle preservation isn't a neutral choice.

Talk to your prescribing physician about protein targets and ask for a referral to someone who specializes in exercise for older adults if you can find one. The medication may be working exactly as intended. It just needs a partner.
