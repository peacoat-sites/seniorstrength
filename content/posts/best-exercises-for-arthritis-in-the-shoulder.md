Rotator cuff pain, clicking, that deep ache when you reach for a seat belt, shoulder arthritis has a way of making ordinary moments feel like decisions. The good news is that movement, done right, is one of the most effective things you can do for it. Not rest. Not avoidance. Movement, with the right exercises and a clear understanding of why they help.

The American College of Rheumatology and the National Institute on Aging both point toward regular, targeted exercise as a first-line approach for managing osteoarthritis pain and preserving function. That applies to the shoulder just as much as the knee or hip, even though the shoulder gets far less attention in mainstream arthritis guidance. The goal isn't to rebuild the joint from scratch. It's to keep the muscles around it strong enough to offload stress from cartilage, maintain enough range of motion to stay functional, and reduce the stiffness that makes pain worse.

One thing to establish before going further: if you're dealing with an acute flare, significant swelling, or new pain you haven't had evaluated, see your doctor or a physical therapist before starting a new exercise program. The exercises here are grounded in what's well-supported for shoulder osteoarthritis, but the right starting point depends on your specific diagnosis.

## Why the Shoulder Is Different

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, and that mobility comes at a cost: it's inherently less stable than the hip. The rotator cuff, four muscles that wrap around the joint, does a large share of the stabilizing work. In shoulder osteoarthritis, those muscles often become inhibited by pain and gradually weaken, which creates a cycle: weaker cuff means more load on the joint surface, more pain, more guarding, more weakness.

This matters for exercise selection because the standard "do some arm circles and call it a day" approach misses the cuff almost entirely. Arm circles, shoulder rolls, and generic stretching address range of motion. They don't address the neuromuscular weakness that's driving a lot of the dysfunction. You need both, and they're not interchangeable.

## Range-of-Motion Exercises That Actually Work

Start here, every session. The goal is to warm the joint, reduce morning stiffness, and maintain whatever range you currently have.

**Pendulum swings.** Stand next to a table, lean forward, and place your uninvolved hand on the table for support. Let the affected arm hang freely. Use a small hip sway to get the arm moving in gentle circles, first clockwise, then counterclockwise, without actively contracting the shoulder muscles. The arm should feel like a pendulum, not like you're steering it. Twenty rotations each direction is a reasonable starting point. This is widely used in post-surgical rehab because it decompresses the joint and gets synovial fluid moving without loading the cuff.

**Supine passive flexion.** Lie on your back and use your unaffected arm (or a cane, or a light dowel) to lift the affected arm overhead, as far as it goes without pain or significant resistance. This is a passive stretch, meaning the muscles around the painful shoulder aren't doing the lifting. You're lengthening them without triggering protective contraction. Hold five to ten seconds at the end of range, lower slowly, repeat six to ten times.

**Wall walks.** Stand facing a wall, place your fingertips on it at shoulder height, and slowly walk them upward as high as you can comfortably go. You'll likely feel mild tension in the capsule at the end of range. Hold two to three seconds, walk back down. The wall gives feedback about whether you're hiking your shoulder blade rather than actually moving the arm, which is a common compensation pattern that reinforces dysfunction.

Do these daily. Mornings are often best because they address overnight stiffness, but anytime works. Keep sessions short, ten to fifteen minutes of range-of-motion work is enough. Longer doesn't mean better.

## The Strengthening Work Most People Skip

This is where the real joint protection happens, and it's the section most generic arthritis advice leaves out or treats too briefly.

The rotator cuff muscles, supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis, need to be trained specifically. The larger muscles like the deltoid and trapezius will naturally do more work if the cuff is weak, which raises joint compression forces rather than lowering them. You want to train the stabilizers first.

**Side-lying external rotation.** Lie on your unaffected side, the affected arm on top, elbow bent to 90 degrees. Hold a light weight (even a can of soup is fine to start). Keeping the elbow pinned to your side, rotate the forearm upward toward the ceiling. Control the return. The movement range is small, often only 45 degrees. That's correct. This is one of the highest-yield exercises you can do for shoulder arthritis because infraspinatus and teres minor are almost always underworked and their weakness directly increases superior migration of the humeral head, meaning the ball rides higher in the socket and increases contact stress on worn cartilage.

**Band internal rotation.** Anchor a resistance band at elbow height. Stand sideways to the anchor, the affected arm closer to the wall. Elbow at 90 degrees, upper arm against your side. Pull the band inward across your body. Control the return. This trains subscapularis, the front of the cuff, which is often neglected because people associate "shoulder exercises" with pushing movements.

**Scapular squeezes.** Sit tall and gently draw your shoulder blades together and slightly downward. Hold three seconds, release. No weight needed. This matters because the scapula is the foundation the shoulder joint moves on, and weak or poorly timed scapular stabilizers put abnormal stress on the glenohumeral joint with every arm movement.

Aim for two to three sessions per week of strengthening. Start with one set of ten to twelve reps and build from there. Resistance bands are often easier to control than dumbbells and allow you to modify resistance more gradually, which is worth knowing when you're managing an irritable joint.

## Water Exercise: The Most Underrated Option

Warm water, typically around 83 to 88 degrees Fahrenheit, which is what most therapy pools maintain, reduces pain signals, allows greater range of motion, and provides resistance without the compressive forces of gravity-based exercise. The Arthritis Foundation's aquatic program, often called the Arthritis Foundation Aquatic Program (AFAP), is an evidence-based group class specifically designed for people with arthritis. Many YMCAs and hospital wellness centers run it. It's worth looking up whether there's one near you.

If a structured class isn't available, exercising in a warm pool on your own works. Shoulder circles, arm sweeps forward and back, gentle pulling motions through the water, these load the joint lightly and can be done on days when dry-land exercises feel like too much.

The research here isn't perfect (most studies are small and short-term), but the direction is consistent: aquatic exercise reduces pain and improves function in people with shoulder and upper-extremity arthritis. It's particularly useful during flares when land-based strengthening isn't tolerable.

## What ACSM Actually Recommends

The American College of Sports Medicine's guidelines for exercise with arthritis call for 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity, plus two to three days of strength training, plus range-of-motion work on most days. That's the same recommendation they make for healthy older adults, which is intentional. Arthritis doesn't mean you get a reduced prescription. It means you need to be more thoughtful about how you get there.

For the shoulder specifically, those guidelines don't specify exercises, but the American Physical Therapy Association and the Arthritis Foundation both publish shoulder-specific guidance that aligns with what's described here. The CDC's "Arthritis Physical Activity" program page is a good starting point if you want an overview of evidence-based activity programs in your area.

| Exercise Type | Frequency | Intensity Signal | Main Benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pendulum swings | Daily | No pain, passive movement only | Joint decompression, fluid circulation |
| Supine passive flexion | Daily | Mild stretch, not pain | Maintains overhead range |
| Wall walks | Daily | Mild tension at end range | Range of motion, scapular awareness |
| External/internal rotation (band or light dumbbell) | 2-3x/week | Mild fatigue at rep 10-12 | Rotator cuff strength, joint stability |
| Scapular squeezes | 2-3x/week | Mild muscle engagement | Scapular stability, posture |
| Aquatic exercise | 2-3x/week | Comfortable, low-load | Pain reduction, range, light strengthening |

## The Pain Rule

Exercise should not be painless. Some muscle fatigue, mild achiness, and a low-level sense of effort are normal and expected. The threshold most physical therapists use, and that the Arthritis Foundation explicitly references in its exercise guidance, is a pain scale of about 4 out of 10 during exercise, with symptoms returning to baseline within an hour after you finish.

If pain during exercise exceeds 4 out of 10, reduce your range of motion or resistance. Don't stop entirely unless pain is severe or sharp. If pain from a session is still elevated the next morning, you did too much and need to dial back.

What you don't want to do is rest completely at the first sign of discomfort. That's the most common mistake in self-managed shoulder arthritis, and it accelerates both weakness and stiffness. The joint needs movement. The question is calibrating how much.

## Common Advice That Doesn't Help

A few things circulate widely but are either unsupported or potentially counterproductive:

Aggressive stretching into pain. Stretching to the point of significant discomfort doesn't speed recovery in an arthritic joint. It can trigger protective muscle spasm and leave the joint more irritated than it was before. Stretch to mild tension, hold, release.

Using heat alone without movement. Heat feels good and does reduce muscle tension. But heat without subsequent movement loses most of its therapeutic benefit. Warm up, then move.

Only doing big compound movements. Bench presses, shoulder presses, and rows all have their place, but if you're not specifically targeting the rotator cuff, you're probably strengthening the muscles that already overpower it. The balance matters more than the total volume.

Assuming more pain means more damage. Activity-related pain in osteoarthritis doesn't reliably predict structural deterioration. Some people with significant cartilage loss on imaging have mild pain; others with less structural change are quite symptomatic. This is worth knowing because fear of "making things worse" keeps a lot of people more sedentary than they need to be.
