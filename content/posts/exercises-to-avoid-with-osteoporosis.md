Three years ago, a woman I'll call Margaret walked into a physical therapy clinic where I was consulting, carrying a printout from a popular fitness website. She'd been doing sit-ups every morning to "strengthen her core" after her doctor told her she had osteoporosis. She was proud of her consistency. Four months of it.

The printout was wrong. And nobody had told her.

That moment is why this topic matters so much. Osteoporosis advice online is a mess of outdated recommendations, vague warnings, and well-meaning but dangerous suggestions from sources that don't actually specialize in bone health. So let me be direct about what to skip, why it matters, and what you can do instead.

## Why the wrong exercise is genuinely risky here

Most fitness mistakes cost you time or soreness. With osteoporosis, a bad exercise choice can cost you a vertebra.

That sounds alarming, and I'm not saying it to scare you into paralysis. I'm saying it because understanding the actual mechanism helps you make better decisions, not just follow rules you don't understand.

Osteoporotic bone has reduced density and altered architecture. The trabecular bone (the spongy inner structure, especially in your spine and hips) becomes more porous, which means it handles compressive and shear forces differently than healthy bone. A vertebral compression fracture can happen without a fall. It can happen during a forward bend, a sudden sneeze, or yes, a crunch. The T12-L1 junction in your lower thoracic spine is one of the most vulnerable spots, and it's exactly the area that takes the most load during spinal flexion under any kind of resistance.

Here's what most people don't realize: many fractures from osteoporosis don't cause immediate sharp pain. They're "silent" fractures that show up on imaging months later. By then, the person has often continued the activity that caused the problem. The feedback loop that normally teaches us to stop doing something harmful doesn't work the way it usually does.

Your doctor may have told you to "be careful" and "[avoid falls](/home-safety-tips-to-prevent-senior-falls/)." That's necessary but nowhere near sufficient. Let's get specific.

## The exercises I'd cut first

**Spinal flexion under load.** This is the big one. Sit-ups, crunches, the "hundred" in Pilates, toe touches with a rounded back, and any seated machine crunch at the gym. All of these put your thoracic and lumbar spine into flexion while adding load or momentum. If you have established osteoporosis, especially in the spine, these are the exercises I'd remove immediately.

Yoga is wonderful for flexibility and stress reduction, but several common poses are flexion-heavy. Child's pose, seated forward fold, and certain twisting poses all involve significant spinal flexion or rotation under load. I've seen yoga instructors who are excellent at modifying these for people with bone density concerns, and I've seen instructors who had no idea this was even a consideration. Don't assume your instructor knows. Ask, and be specific about your diagnosis.

**High-impact activities without appropriate preparation.** Running, jumping jacks, jump rope, plyometrics, and step aerobics classes with jump components all generate ground reaction forces that can be two to five times your body weight. This sounds counterintuitive because impact loading is actually good for bone density in healthy adults, and there's real evidence for it. The problem with osteoporosis is that the bone may not be in a condition to handle high, sudden loads safely, particularly if you've had previous fractures or if your T-score is significantly below -2.5. A 2022 systematic review in Osteoporosis International looked at this specifically and found that low-to-moderate impact loading (walking, low-step aerobics) generally benefits bone, while high-impact jumping in those with established osteoporosis carries meaningful fracture risk without proportional benefit.

The line here isn't perfectly crisp, and I'll be honest: the research on impact thresholds is still developing. Your bone density score, fracture history, and overall balance all factor in. This is genuinely a conversation to have with your doctor or a physical therapist who specializes in osteoporosis, not something to self-prescribe.

**Loaded spinal rotation and heavy trunk twisting.** Golf swings, baseball batting practice, and certain cable machine rotational exercises create torque in the spine that osteoporotic vertebrae handle poorly. Recreational golf is a surprisingly common trigger for vertebral fractures in this population. If you love golf, modifications exist (shorter swing arc, avoiding the extreme rotational follow-through), but playing the full game with no changes after an osteoporosis diagnosis is something I'd push back on hard.

## The sit-up problem deserves its own section

I keep coming back to this because it's the most common mistake I see, and it's in so many "senior fitness" programs that were written without osteoporosis in mind.

The sit-up and the crunch both involve repeated, loaded flexion of the lumbar and thoracic spine. Even bodyweight versions. Biomechanist Dr. Stuart McGill at the University of Waterloo has spent decades documenting the compressive loads on spinal discs during these exercises, and his numbers are sobering even for people with healthy spines. For someone with reduced bone density in the vertebral bodies, that force equation gets significantly worse.

The substitute isn't "do nothing for your core." Your core absolutely needs to be strong, arguably more than ever, because strong core musculature protects and supports your spine. The substitute is anti-flexion, anti-extension, and anti-rotation core work: exercises like the McGill curl-up (very small movement, neutral spine), the dead bug, the bird-dog, and planks done with good form. These build real functional core stability without repeatedly loading the spine into flexion.

I want to address the "but I've always done sit-ups and I'm fine" response, because I hear it constantly. Silent fractures. They don't always announce themselves. And "fine so far" is different from "safe."

## Machines and equipment worth reconsidering

Leg press machines at high weight: the combination of spinal compression in the seated position plus the compressive load from heavy pressing is worth questioning. Light-to-moderate weight is a different story.

The seated rowing machine and the lat pulldown both involve forward trunk lean or spinal loading in ways that can be problematic if your thoracic spine density is already compromised.

Resistance bands are genuinely excellent here, by the way, because you control the resistance incrementally and the force profiles are more forgiving. The TheraBand Resistance Band set (around $15-25) is something I've recommended to dozens of people as a starting point for home strengthening work.

Pool exercises and water aerobics are underrated for this population. The buoyancy reduces fracture risk from falls while still allowing meaningful resistance work. Not every gym has a pool, I know. But if yours does, it's worth exploring.

## What I actually want you to take away from this

Avoid high-impact without appropriate clearance. Avoid spinal flexion under load. Be thoughtful about deep spinal rotation. And please, please don't let a vague fear of "doing something wrong" push you toward doing nothing.

Inactivity is a real risk with osteoporosis too. Muscle weakness leads to falls. Falls lead to fractures. The goal is calibrated, appropriate exercise, not avoidance.

Walking, strength training with proper technique, Tai Chi (a 2020 Cochrane review found meaningful evidence for Tai Chi reducing fall risk in older adults), balance work, and modified weight-bearing exercises can all be done safely and beneficially with osteoporosis. You just need a program built for your specific situation.

Which brings me to the most important recommendation I can make: get a physical therapy evaluation from someone who works regularly with osteoporosis. One or two PT sessions to build a safe, personalized program is worth far more than months of YouTube workouts that weren't designed with your bone density in mind. Many insurance plans, including Medicare, cover PT visits for this, so check your benefits before assuming it's out of pocket.

#### Is walking safe with osteoporosis?

Yes. Walking is generally one of the safest and most recommended exercises for people with osteoporosis. It's weight-bearing, which provides mild mechanical loading that supports bone health, and the impact forces are low enough to be appropriate for most people even with significant bone density loss. Walking on uneven surfaces requires some caution if your balance is compromised.

#### Can I still do yoga if I have osteoporosis?

You can, but you'll need to modify or skip poses that involve deep spinal flexion (forward folds), heavy rotation, or positions that put you at fall risk. Look specifically for instructors who have training in osteoporosis-safe yoga. The book "Yoga for Osteoporosis" by Loren Fishman, MD is a legitimately good resource and probably the most evidence-backed approach to this I've come across.

#### My bone density T-score is -1.8. Do I need to be as careful as someone with a score of -3.0?

A T-score of -1.8 is in the osteopenia range, which is lower than normal but not yet osteoporosis. The precautions are generally less strict, but spinal flexion under load and high-impact jumping are still worth limiting, especially if you have other risk factors like a history of falls, steroid use, or small frame size. Discuss your specific numbers with your doctor, because fracture risk involves more than just the T-score.

#### Is swimming good for osteoporosis?

Swimming is safe and great for cardiovascular health, muscle strength, and joint mobility, but it doesn't provide the weight-bearing stimulus that bones need to maintain or build density. It's a fine part of your fitness routine, but it shouldn't be the only thing you're doing if bone health is the goal. Water aerobics with resistance work is a better option than lap swimming alone.

#### Should I stop exercising after a vertebral fracture?

Not necessarily, but you absolutely need medical clearance and professional guidance before resuming. The acute phase after a fracture requires careful management, and the exercises you do during recovery look very different from a general maintenance program. A physical therapist working with your orthopedic or rheumatology team is the right person to help structure this. Don't try to self-manage a post-fracture exercise return based on online information alone, including this article.
