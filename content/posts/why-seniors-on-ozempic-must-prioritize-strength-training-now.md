If you're over 60 and you've started taking Ozempic, Wegovy, or another GLP-1 medication, you're probably feeling some version of cautious optimism right now. The weight is moving. Your doctor seems pleased. Maybe your knees feel a little better going up stairs. What you might not be expecting is a warning that arrives not from your scale, but from your muscles, and it's one that a major piece of new research is making impossible to ignore.

At the Endocrine Society's ENDO 2026 conference in Chicago on June 14, researchers presented findings that should be landing on every prescribing physician's radar. Using Fitbit data linked to the NIH's All of Us Research Program, the study found that people who started GLP-1 medications took 560 fewer steps per day and did nearly six fewer minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity every day after beginning treatment. That's not a rounding error. That's a meaningful, measurable drop in the movement that older bodies depend on to stay functional.

For adults over 60, this compounds a problem that was already serious before anyone started talking about step counts.

## The Muscle Loss Nobody Warned You About

Here's what I tell people when they come to me confused about why they feel weaker even as the number on the scale drops: GLP-1 drugs don't just take fat. Research consistently estimates that 20 to 40 percent of the weight lost on medications like semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, and tirzepatide comes from lean tissue, meaning muscle. That's a wide range, and the exact number varies by individual, but even the low end of that estimate matters enormously for someone who is 65 or 70 and already starting from a depleted baseline.

The clinical term for what many older adults in this situation are dealing with is sarcopenic obesity: carrying excess fat while simultaneously having insufficient muscle mass. A June 2026 review in PMC estimates this condition affects roughly 20 percent of obese adults seeking treatment, and it creates a cruel paradox. These are often the people who need GLP-1 therapy most. They're also the people least able to absorb the muscle loss it causes without real consequences, things like reduced walking speed, trouble rising from a chair, and sharply elevated [fall risk](/what-your-grip-strength-says-about-how-long-you-will-live/).

You might be wondering whether the muscle loss is just a cosmetic concern, something you'd notice in the mirror but that wouldn't affect your daily life. It isn't. Muscle is your primary defense against falls. It regulates blood sugar. It supports your joints. It is, functionally, your independence. Losing it quietly while the scale ticks downward is not a trade-off worth making passively.

## Why Moving Less Is the Part That Surprised Researchers

The ENDO 2026 findings, reported by both the Endocrine Society and covered in Science Daily on June 14, point to something that wasn't on most clinicians' radar when they started prescribing these medications at scale. GLP-1 drugs suppress appetite, which reduces caloric intake. But they may also be reducing the drive to move. The mechanism isn't fully understood yet, but the effect in the data was clear and consistent across participants.

Think about what 560 fewer steps per day actually adds up to. Over a week, that's nearly 4,000 steps. Over a month, it's close to 17,000. For an older adult who is already contending with arthritis, balance concerns, or just the normal slowing that comes with age, that invisible reduction in daily movement accelerates exactly the [muscle and bone loss](/ozempic-and-muscle-loss-what-older-adults-on-glp-1s-must-know/) the medication was already triggering. It's two forces pulling in the same direction, and neither one is on your side.

About 12 percent of Americans are currently using or have previously used GLP-1 therapies. That makes this a mainstream public health issue, not a niche clinical footnote. And because older adults are increasingly in that 12 percent, the conversation about exercise as a non-negotiable companion to these medications needs to get louder and more specific.

The good news is that this isn't a problem without a solution. It's a problem without enough urgency around the solution. Researchers at Rice University and the Methodist Hospital Research Institute launched a clinical trial in April 2026 (registered as NCT07554417 on ClinicalTrials.gov) specifically designed to test whether a structured 12-week exercise and nutrition program can counter the muscle and bone loss happening in GLP-1 users. The trial is actively running. We don't have results yet, but the fact that this research exists tells you something important: the scientific community recognizes that exercise isn't optional here. It's corrective medicine.

What does "[strength training](/how-much-strength-training-do-seniors-actually-need-to-live-longer/)" actually mean in this context? For most people over 60, it means two to three sessions per week of resistance work, using bodyweight, resistance bands, cable machines, or free weights, focused on the major muscle groups. Squats, supported or otherwise. Rows. Hip hinges. Pressing movements. These aren't exotic exercises. They're the movements your body needs to get up from a chair, carry groceries, and stay upright when the sidewalk isn't perfectly level.

Protein intake matters here too. If you're eating significantly less because of appetite suppression, but you're not prioritizing protein within those reduced calories, you're giving your body almost no raw material to preserve or rebuild muscle. Most older adults need more protein than general dietary guidelines suggest, typically in the range of 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, especially when they're losing weight. This is something worth discussing specifically with your physician or a registered dietitian who works with older adults.

## What the Fitness World Is Recognizing Right Now

It's not just clinical researchers paying attention. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 Worldwide Fitness Trends report, based on a survey of 2,000 clinicians and researchers, ranked fitness programs for older adults as the number two global fitness trend for 2026. That's a significant signal. The people who study and practice exercise medicine professionally are watching what's happening with this population and responding to it.

The ranking reflects something I see in the gym and in clinical settings: there's growing recognition that structured, supervised exercise for older adults isn't a soft wellness add-on. It's a medical intervention. And for someone on a GLP-1 drug, it may be the most important one you're not doing.

If you've been told by your doctor to "stay active" while on Ozempic and left the appointment without anything more specific than that, you're not alone. Many prescribers are still calibrating their guidance on the exercise side of this equation. Pushing for specifics, asking about referrals to physical therapy or certified trainers who work with older adults, and bringing up your concerns about muscle loss by name are all reasonable and productive steps.

The medications can do real work. But right now, in June 2026, the evidence is pointing clearly in one direction: they work best when you're working too. If your activity has quietly dropped since starting treatment, you're not failing. You're responding normally to a drug that may be making movement feel less urgent. The research just showed us that. The question now is what you do with that information.

Please consult your physician, a registered dietitian, and a qualified fitness professional before beginning or modifying any exercise program, particularly if you have existing health conditions or mobility limitations.
