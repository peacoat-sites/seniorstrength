Most advice about core training for older adults is wrong. Not dangerous, exactly, just wrong in a way that wastes time and sometimes makes things worse. The standard recommendation you'll hear at most gyms goes something like this: "Do some planks, maybe a few crunches, try a bird dog." And sure, those exercises exist on a spectrum somewhere between fine and useful. But they're almost never taught in the right order, for the right reasons, and they skip over the foundational work that actually makes a difference when you're 65, 72, or 84.

I spent years defaulting to the same cookie-cutter progressions before I started paying closer attention to what was actually happening with my older clients. What surprised me was how often someone would come in after months of doing "core work" at their previous gym and still have almost no ability to generate stability when standing on one leg, reaching overhead, or getting up from a low chair. They'd done hundreds of crunches. It hadn't translated.

Here's why that happens, and what to do instead.

## What "Core" Actually Means After 60

Let's clear something up. The core isn't your abs. Or rather, it's not *only* your abs. The system includes your deep spinal muscles (the multifidus, the erector spinae), your pelvic floor, your diaphragm, and the transverse abdominis, which wraps around your midsection like a corset. These muscles don't work by producing big movements. They work by creating pressure and stiffness to protect the spine and transfer force between your legs and arms.

After 60, this distinction matters more than most people realize. Research published in *The Journals of Gerontology* (2016 and several follow-up studies) has pretty consistently found that deep spinal muscle mass and coordination decline with age, often before people notice anything going wrong. The multifidus in particular shows significant atrophy in older adults, even ones who are otherwise active. Doing crunches doesn't train the multifidus. Not even close.

Then there's the fall problem. The CDC reports that one in four Americans over 65 falls each year, and fall-related injuries are a leading cause of both mortality and loss of independence in that population. Good core training doesn't just make your waist look better. It changes the reflexive stability that kicks in when you stumble off a curb or when someone bumps you in a grocery store. That's what we're actually chasing.

## The Foundation: Breathing and Bracing

Breathing doesn't sound like an exercise. Bear with me.

A huge percentage of older adults have a dysfunctional breathing pattern where the chest rises before the belly expands. This basically means the diaphragm isn't descending properly and the deep core system never gets the pressure signal it needs to activate. Before you do a single plank, spend two weeks just relearning how to breathe into your belly, into your sides, and then feel the gentle outward pressure around your whole midsection as you exhale slowly.

The technique you're working toward is called an "abdominal brace." Not a suck-in. A brace, like you're about to absorb a light punch. Think about gently tensing every muscle around your midsection simultaneously, about 30 to 40 percent of your maximum effort. That's the baseline activation you want to learn before everything else.

Here's the practice: lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Breathe in through your nose. On the exhale, brace gently and hold for 5 seconds while continuing to breathe normally. That combination, bracing while still breathing, is where a lot of people struggle at first. It takes a few sessions before it feels natural, and that's okay. Once you can do it lying down, practice it seated, then standing.

This isn't just warmup fluff. A 2018 paper in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* found that proper bracing technique significantly improves force transfer and spinal stability during loaded movements. If you're skipping this step and jumping straight to planks, you're missing the part that actually makes the planks do something useful.

## The Exercises That Actually Work

Once you've got the brace dialed in, the progression I've found most reliable with older clients goes roughly like this, moving from horizontal to vertical, from simple to complex.

**Dead Bug**

This one gets underused because it doesn't look impressive. It is impressive, especially for anyone with a history of low back pain. Lie on your back, arms pointing straight up toward the ceiling, knees bent at 90 degrees with shins parallel to the floor. Brace your core, press your lower back gently into the mat, and then slowly lower one arm overhead and the opposite leg toward the floor simultaneously. Return. Switch sides.

The key is that your lower back should not arch away from the mat at any point. If it does, you've gone too far. Shorten the range. Start with 5 reps per side and build from there.

**Bird Dog**

You may have heard of this one, but most people do it wrong. Start on hands and knees. Brace first. Then extend one arm and the opposite leg until they're roughly parallel to the floor. Hold for 3 to 5 seconds. Return slowly. The mistake most people make is extending the arm and leg while letting the hips rotate and the lower back sag. If you put a foam roller across your lower back while doing this, it shouldn't roll off. That's your cue.

People with wrist issues can do a modified version from a bench or a countertop.

**Seated Marching**

Don't underestimate this one. Sit tall in a firm chair, feet flat on the floor. Brace your core, then lift one knee slowly until your foot is a few inches off the ground. Hold 3 seconds. Lower. Repeat on the other side. No gripping the chair. What you're training here is hip flexor strength in a functional position and the anti-rotation demand of keeping your trunk still as one side becomes unsupported. It's a bridge between floor exercises and standing movement patterns, and for clients who struggle to get down to the floor, it's often where we start.

**Standing Anti-Rotation Press**

You'll need a resistance band anchored to a door handle or a sturdy post at about chest height. Stand sideways to the anchor point, feet shoulder-width apart. Hold the band with both hands at your chest, elbows bent, then press straight out in front of you slowly, hold 2 seconds, and return. The force is trying to rotate your torso toward the anchor. Your job is to resist that without holding your breath.

This exercise is about as functional as core training gets. It mirrors the demands of nearly every real-world movement: reaching for something across your body, catching yourself from falling, carrying a bag on one side. A set of TheraBand CLX bands runs around $15 to $20 and is sufficient for most people starting out.

**Modified Plank (on Knees or from a Bench)**

Yes, planks belong here. Just not as the starting point. Once the other work is established, a plank from the knees (forearms down, knees down, body in a straight line from head to knees) is an excellent isometric hold. Build from 10-second holds toward 30, then 45. Keep the hips from rising or sagging. Keep breathing.

The full plank from toes is appropriate for some people over 60, but if you have wrist, shoulder, or knee pain, it's often not worth chasing. The knee version loads the core just fine.

## Common Mistakes Worth Knowing About

Holding your breath is the most common one. It's also the most important to fix. Breath-holding spikes blood pressure, sometimes dramatically, and creates a false stability that your body doesn't learn to reproduce when it matters. Every exercise above should be done while breathing.

Moving too fast is the second. Slow, controlled movement is where the neurological adaptation happens. If you're rushing through bird dogs or dead bugs, you're training momentum, not stability.

The third mistake is more of a system issue: training core in isolation and never connecting it to functional movement. A Tuesday session of floor exercises is great. But if you can also practice the abdominal brace while walking, while carrying groceries, while standing at the kitchen counter, you're building something that actually sticks.

## How Often, How Much

Three days a week of dedicated core work is plenty for most people, with at least one rest day between sessions. More is not better here, especially with deep stabilizer muscles that need recovery time. A session doesn't need to be long: 15 to 20 minutes of focused, well-executed work is more valuable than an hour of going through the motions.

Progress slowly. The research on older adult motor learning, including a useful review from *Physical Therapy* journal in 2020, suggests that neurological adaptation in this population is absolutely possible but tends to happen over a longer timeline than in younger adults. Expect to spend 4 to 6 weeks on foundational movements before the patterns become automatic.

If you've had a recent back surgery, a compression fracture, significant osteoporosis, or any history of spinal instability, please work with a physical therapist before starting any of this. Some of these exercises genuinely require modification in those situations, and the right starting point looks very different.

---

The real goal here isn't a stronger core for its own sake. It's the ability to stay in your own home, to pick up your grandkids, to hike a trail at 75 without your back giving out two days later. Those outcomes are available to most people who do this work consistently. What makes the difference is starting with the right foundation rather than the exercises that just *look* like they're doing something.
