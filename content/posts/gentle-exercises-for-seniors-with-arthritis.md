Somewhere around the third time you cancel plans because your hands hurt too much to grip the steering wheel, you start wondering if this is just how it goes now.

I've heard that from dozens of clients over the years, and I want to be direct with you: it doesn't have to be. Arthritis is genuinely limiting, but movement is one of the most effective tools we have for managing it. The problem is that a lot of the exercise advice floating around for "seniors with arthritis" is either so vague it's useless or so intense it backfires by the second session. You don't need a cheerful list of "10 easy moves!" You need to understand what's actually happening in your joints and why certain movement patterns help while others flare you up.

I've been working with [adults over](/how-exercise-fights-inflammaging-in-adults-over-60/) 60 for more than fifteen years, split between physical therapy clinic settings and personal training, and the single most common mistake I see is people treating rest as the safe option. It feels logical. Your knee aches, you sit down, the aching backs off. But that relief is temporary, and the inactivity that follows makes the underlying problem worse. Cartilage doesn't have its own blood supply. It gets nutrition from the synovial fluid that surrounds it, and that fluid moves through compression and release during movement. Rest your joint entirely and you're literally starving the tissue you're trying to protect.

## What "gentle" actually means

This word gets misused constantly. I thought for years that "gentle" meant low resistance and low intensity. What I've come to understand is that it really means low-impact and joint-conscious, which is a completely different thing. You can have a gentle [workout that](/the-4-minute-daily-workout-that-builds-strength-after-65/) genuinely challenges your muscles. You can have a "gentle" stretch class that quietly wrecks your inflamed SI joint if the instructor doesn't understand arthritis patterns.

The distinction that matters is joint loading. High-impact activities create ground reaction forces that spike pressure through cartilage surfaces. Running, for example, can load your knee joint at four to eight times your body weight with each stride. That's not the environment you want when the joint surface is already compromised. But [resistance training](/what-the-new-acsm-resistance-training-guidelines-mean-after-60/) with controlled, smooth movements at a comfortable range? That's a different story. A 2017 study published in the *Annals of Internal Medicine* followed 340 adults over 60 with knee osteoarthritis through a progressive strength training program and found that participants who stuck with it reported 43% less pain and significantly better function than the comparison group at 18 months.

Gentle means respecting your current range of motion. Not pushing past where the joint stops comfortably. It means choosing surfaces, equipment, and speeds that keep joint forces manageable. It does not mean puttering around with a two-pound dumbbell forever.

## The exercises that actually work

Let me walk you through what the evidence supports and what I'd actually prescribe to a new client with arthritis. I'll focus on the most common presentation: osteoarthritis in the knees, hips, or hands, which makes up the vast majority of cases in the 60+ population.

**Seated leg raises.** Sit upright in a firm chair (not a soft couch), feet flat. Slowly straighten one knee until your leg is roughly parallel to the floor, hold for three seconds, lower slowly. That's it. Sounds almost embarrassingly simple. But the quadriceps muscles are the primary stabilizers of the knee joint, and even modest strengthening here has a measurable effect on pain and function. Start with two sets of ten on each side. The small detail most people miss: the lowering phase matters as much as the lifting. Control it. Don't let the leg drop.

**Wall slides (partial squats).** Stand with your back against a smooth wall, feet about shoulder-width apart and roughly eighteen inches away from the baseboard. Slowly slide down until you feel your thighs starting to engage, which is typically only a 20-30 degree bend for most beginners with knee OA. Hold for five seconds. Slide back up. This is not a full squat and should not be. Your knees should not travel past your toes. If you feel sharp pain rather than muscle effort, you've gone too far. A small thing that matters here: wear socks on a hardwood floor or put a towel between your back and the wall so you actually slide cleanly.

**Standing hip abduction with chair support.** Hold the back of a sturdy chair with both hands. Slowly lift one leg out to the side, about 12-18 inches, keeping your torso upright. Don't lean. Lower it with control. This targets the hip abductors (your gluteus medius, specifically), which are chronically weak in people with hip OA and contribute enormously to that "giving way" feeling when going down stairs. Three sets of twelve, twice a week, and I've watched clients go from white-knuckling the handrail to walking downstairs naturally within about six weeks.

**Warm water movement.** If you have access to a pool, a hot tub, or even a warm bath, use it. Water at roughly 92-96°F reduces muscle guarding, the involuntary tension your body creates around a painful joint, and lets you move through ranges you'd otherwise resist on land. Walk forward and backward across the shallow end. March in place. Gently circle your ankles. The Arthritis Foundation has offered Aquatic Exercise programs through YMCA branches nationwide for years, and as of 2026 many YMCAs charge as little as $10-15 per session for non-members or include it in membership rates around $40-55 per month for seniors. It's one of the best values in senior fitness, honestly.

**Finger and wrist mobility for hand arthritis.** Make a loose fist, then open your hand fully and spread your fingers wide. Do this ten times. Follow it with gentle wrist circles, five in each direction. Then try the "tabletop" position: place your hand flat on a table and, one finger at a time, lift each finger off the surface while keeping the others down. This sounds trivial until you realize that maintaining dexterity and grip strength is directly tied to independence. Opening a jar, managing buttons, holding a book. These matter.

## The morning stiffness problem

Here's what most people don't realize about morning stiffness: it's predictable, it's manageable, and waiting it out in bed is actually one of the worst strategies.

Synovial fluid, which cushions and feeds your joints, pools and thickens overnight when you're not moving. That's why the first twenty minutes after you wake up feel like you're made of rusted hinges. The solution isn't to lie there hoping it improves. Gentle movement within the first five to ten minutes of waking accelerates the warming and redistribution of that fluid.

Before you even get out of bed, try this: pump your ankles up and down twenty times. Gently bend and straighten each knee, just in the range that's comfortable. Slowly bring each knee toward your chest and hold for five seconds. Roll your wrists. These take about four minutes total and make a noticeable difference by the time you get to the bathroom. A client of mine, a 74-year-old retired teacher named Margaret who has moderate OA in both knees, told me that adding this routine cut her "can't-walk-right-yet" window from about 45 minutes down to roughly 10. That's not nothing.

## What to do on a flare day

Stop. I know the research on movement is compelling. I know you've probably just started building a routine and the last thing you want to do is break it. But a flare, meaning a joint that's genuinely hot, swollen, and significantly more painful than your baseline, is your body asking for a different kind of attention.

On a flare day: ice or heat (whichever you personally respond better to, the research here is genuinely mixed and individual preference matters), gentle range-of-motion only (not strengthening), and elevation if swelling is present. That's the short list. If a flare lasts more than three days, or is dramatically worse than what you normally experience, call your doctor. Don't try to exercise through it and don't assume you know what's happening.

The moment I would most want to take back in my own practice was pushing a client through what she described as "worse than usual" knee pain. I thought I knew what her baseline felt like. It turned out to be a septic joint that needed emergency treatment. I don't say that to scare you. I say it because severity cues matter, and a physical therapist or physician is the right person to help you distinguish your normal arthritis discomfort from something that needs medical attention.

## A realistic starting point

Here's what a first week might look like for someone starting from zero with mild-to-moderate knee OA:

Day one: Seated leg raises, two sets of ten each side. Seated ankle pumps, twenty reps. Total time: about eight minutes.

Day two: A ten-minute walk at a comfortable pace on a flat surface. If the knee aches during it, that's okay. If it aches significantly worse for more than an hour after, shorten the walk next time.

Day three: Rest or gentle stretching only.

Day four: Add wall slides, two sets of eight. Return to the seated leg raises.

Day five: Ten-to-fifteen minute walk.

Day six and seven: Rest, or gentle morning mobility work in bed.

That's it. That's the whole plan. You're building the habit and learning your body's response before you add intensity. After two weeks with no significant setbacks, add a set or increase the reps. Progress in arthritis exercise is measured in months, not days. That can be frustrating when you're used to faster feedback. But a 2019 meta-analysis in *Osteoarthritis and Cartilage* pooling data from 5,700 participants found that consistent low-to-moderate exercise for twelve weeks produced the greatest improvements in pain and physical function. Twelve weeks. Not two.
