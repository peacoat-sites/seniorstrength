My client Margaret came in one Tuesday morning barely able to grip the door handle. She was 71, had moderate osteoarthritis in both hands and her right knee, and had just been told by a well-meaning friend to "just do some yoga." Margaret had tried. She'd watched a YouTube video, attempted a downward dog, and spent the next two days icing her wrists.

That story isn't unusual. I hear versions of it constantly. The advice floating around for seniors with arthritis tends to be either dangerously vague ("[stay active](/how-to-stay-active-with-arthritis/)!") or copied straight from a general fitness playbook that was never designed for inflamed, stiff, or painful joints. What most people don't realize is that exercising with arthritis isn't just about going gentler. It's about choosing movements that work with your joint mechanics, not against them, and knowing when to back off versus when the discomfort is actually productive.

I've worked with [older adults](/strength-training-is-now-essential-for-older-adults-on-glp-1-drugs/) in physical therapy and personal training settings for over a decade, and I want to give you the real picture: what helps, what the research actually shows, and which common recommendations I'd quietly ignore.

## Why "just move more" is incomplete advice

Here's the thing nobody says out loud: movement is genuinely medicine for arthritis. That part is true. Synovial fluid, which is what lubricates your joints, doesn't circulate well when you're sedentary. Movement pumps it through the joint space. A 2019 study published in *JAMA Internal Medicine* following over 1,500 older adults with knee osteoarthritis found that those who did structured moderate exercise were 40% less likely to develop major mobility disability compared to those who didn't, even after controlling for disease severity.

But "just move more" skips the part where the *type* and *timing* of movement matters enormously. Doing the wrong exercise on an actively inflamed joint doesn't pump synovial fluid. It irritates synovium. There's a meaningful difference.

The practical rule I give every client: if a joint is visibly swollen, hot to the touch, or significantly more painful than your baseline, that's an acute flare. Range-of-motion only during a flare. No resistance, no sustained holds. Everything else in this article applies to your non-flare days, which for most people with osteoarthritis is most of the time.

## The exercises that actually work

Let me be direct about my ranking, because I think it saves time.

**Aquatic exercise is the single best starting point for most people with arthritis.** The water reduces the effective weight load on your joints by up to 90% when you're submerged to shoulder depth. The Arthritis Foundation has been running its Aquatic Program for years, and if you can find a local chapter running classes (many YMCAs partner with them for around $8 to $15 per class), that's worth a call before you spend money on a personal trainer. I've seen people in their 80s with bilateral knee replacements make their first real strength gains in a pool.

**Chair-based strength training** is second, and it's criminally underrated. Sitting doesn't mean easy. A proper seated leg press against a resistance band, or a seated row with a resistance tube anchored in a door, can build meaningful quadriceps and upper back strength without requiring you to stabilize an unstable standing position. For someone with hip arthritis, this distinction matters.

**Tai chi** has about as much research behind it as any exercise modality for arthritis. A 2021 meta-analysis in *Annals of Internal Medicine* looked at 18 randomized controlled trials and found that tai chi produced significant reductions in pain, stiffness, and physical function scores compared to controls. The specific style matters less than you'd think. Yang style, which is the most commonly taught, is fine. Sessions twice a week seem to be the minimum effective dose.

**Walking**, done correctly, is still good. I want to be clear about that because some people interpret "gentle exercise" as meaning they should stop walking. Walking on flat, cushioned surfaces (think a track or a soft trail, not concrete sidewalks) in well-fitted shoes puts manageable compressive force on knee joints and has genuine cardiovascular and bone density benefits. I'd just add: don't walk through sharp, stabbing pain. Dull aching that fades within an hour after you stop? Usually fine. Pain that spikes during the walk and persists for hours after? That's your body asking you to modify.

## A starter weekly framework

I'm going to give you something concrete, because "do some gentle movement most days" is not a plan.

| Day | Activity | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Chair strength (lower body) | 15 min | Seated leg extensions, clam shells, mini squats to chair |
| Tuesday | Aquatic class or gentle walking | 20-30 min | Pool preferred if available |
| Wednesday | Tai chi or yoga (chair-modified) | 20 min | Focus on hip and shoulder mobility |
| Thursday | Rest or gentle stretching only | 10 min | Joint circles, neck rolls, wrist mobility |
| Friday | Chair strength (upper body) | 15 min | Seated row, wall push-ups, shoulder blade squeezes |
| Saturday | Walking or aquatic | 20 min | Aim for flat terrain |
| Sunday | Full rest | -- | Non-negotiable recovery |

This adds up to roughly 100 to 120 minutes of structured activity [per week](/how-much-exercise-do-seniors-need-per-week/), which falls within the CDC's current recommendations (as of July 2026) for older adults with chronic conditions. Starting here and building slowly is smarter than trying to hit the upper range immediately.

One thing I learned the hard way with Margaret: the warm-up isn't optional. I used to let motivated clients skip it when they were short on time. That was a mistake. For arthritis specifically, five minutes of gentle joint circles (wrists, ankles, hips, neck) before any resistance work isn't just injury prevention. It's actually producing the synovial fluid circulation that makes the exercise feel tolerable. Skip it and the first ten minutes of your workout will feel awful.

## The inflammation timing problem

Here's something that surprised me when I first read the research and has since completely changed how I schedule sessions for arthritis clients. Morning stiffness, that feeling of cement in your joints when you first get up, is worst in the first one to two hours after waking. This is partly inflammatory cytokine activity that peaks in early morning.

What that means practically: don't schedule your strength session for 7 a.m. if you wake at 6. Give your joints time to wake up first. Gentle range-of-motion work (literally just moving your joints through their comfortable range while still in bed, or seated on the edge of it) for five to ten minutes before you stand helps. Then a warm shower. Then, once stiffness has reduced, your real exercise session.

A reader named Carol, who emailed me after reading one of my earlier pieces on knee pain, told me she'd been giving up on morning exercise because it "always hurt too much." She shifted her walks from 7 a.m. to 9:30 a.m. and described it as "like exercising with different knees." Same joints, different timing.

Practical worked examples:

Margaret (71, bilateral hand arthritis, right knee OA) → Switched from YouTube yoga to twice-weekly Arthritis Foundation aquatic classes plus a 20-minute chair strength routine → After 8 weeks: grip strength improved measurably on a hand dynamometer, reported 60% reduction in morning stiffness duration

Carol (67, lower back and hip OA) → Shifted walk timing from 7 a.m. to 9:30 a.m., added 5-minute warm-up joint circles → Result: sustained her 5-day walking habit for 4 straight months after previously quitting repeatedly

A group of 12 clients I worked with in a 12-week chair-based strength program → Met twice weekly for 30-minute sessions, no aquatic component → Average self-reported pain score dropped from 6.2/10 to 4.1/10; six of the twelve reduced their NSAID use (in consultation with their physicians)

## What to skip, or at least question

High-intensity interval training: not automatically ruled out, but not where you start. Some people with well-managed arthritis do fine with modified HIIT. Most people beginning an arthritis exercise program don't.

Standing yoga on a mat without props or modifications: I've seen more wrist, wrist, and shoulder irritation come from unmodified sun salutations than from any other single recommendation. Chair yoga is a legitimate alternative, not a consolation prize.

Foam rolling directly over an arthritic joint: the research here is mixed, and I don't have great data specifically for arthritic older adults. Rolling the muscles surrounding a joint (your quads, hamstrings, calves) is generally fine. Rolling directly on a hip or knee that's already inflamed? I'd skip it.
