Arthritis affects roughly 1 in 2 adults over the age of 65. And yet most people's doctor hands them a pamphlet with exercises that look nothing like what their body can actually do that day. If you've ever rolled your eyes at a generic workout recommendation while your knees are swollen and your fingers won't close all the way in the morning, you're not imagining the disconnect. The advice isn't wrong exactly. It's just incomplete.

Here's what I tell people who come to me frustrated, stiff, and not sure whether moving more will help or make things worse: the goal isn't to push through pain. It's to find the edge of what's comfortable and work intelligently just inside it. That edge moves. Some days it's wider than you'd expect. Some days it's much narrower. Both are fine.

## Why Movement Actually Helps (and Why Rest Makes It Worse)

This surprises a lot of people. When a joint hurts, the natural instinct is to protect it, keep it still. And there are absolutely times when that's the right call, especially during an acute flare. But in the long run, prolonged rest leads to muscle atrophy around the joint, reduced synovial fluid circulation (the lubricating fluid in your joints), and increased stiffness. You end up in more pain from resting than you would have been from gentle, appropriate movement.

A 2019 review in the *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews* looked at land-based exercise for knee osteoarthritis and found consistent evidence that exercise reduces pain and improves physical function, even in people with moderate to severe arthritis. The effect sizes weren't huge, but they were real and durable. That tracks with what I see in practice.

The catch is the word "appropriate." Walking five miles when your hip is inflamed is not appropriate. Neither is skipping movement entirely for two weeks because your wrist flared up. The sweet spot, which takes some learning, is low-load movement that promotes circulation, maintains range of motion, and builds the supporting musculature without grinding already-damaged cartilage.

Let me be specific here, because vague guidance like "do gentle stretching" doesn't actually help anyone.

**[Water exercise](/water-aerobics-exercises-for-seniors/).** If you have access to a heated pool, this is the single best environment for arthritis exercise. Full stop. The buoyancy offloads up to 90% of your body weight when you're submerged to the neck, which means your joints get movement with a fraction of the usual compressive load. Warm water between 83 and 88 degrees Fahrenheit also relaxes muscles and reduces joint stiffness before you've done a single rep. The Arthritis Foundation runs Aquatic Exercise for Arthritis (formerly AFYAP) at YMCAs and community pools across the country. It's usually $8 to $15 per class or included in a YMCA membership. Start there before buying any equipment.

**[Chair-based strength work](/chair-exercises-for-seniors/).** Weak muscles around a joint transfer more load directly to the cartilage. Strengthening the quadriceps, for instance, meaningfully reduces knee pain in osteoarthritis patients, and you can do a lot of that work seated. Sit upright in a firm chair, straighten one leg until it's parallel to the floor, hold for three seconds, lower slowly. Ten repetitions, two sets, each side. Sounds almost embarrassingly simple. It works. The key is the slow lowering phase, which loads the muscle eccentrically and builds more functional strength than just lifting and dropping.

**Tai chi.** I used to hesitate recommending this because it felt like I was sending someone to do something gentle instead of something real. I was wrong. A 2016 randomized controlled trial in *Annals of Internal Medicine* compared tai chi to physical therapy for knee osteoarthritis over 52 weeks. Tai chi performed comparably to PT for pain reduction and physical function. It also improved psychological wellbeing and balance in ways PT didn't fully match. If there's a Dr. Paul Lam Tai Chi for Arthritis class near you (search the Arthritis Foundation's class finder), try it for at least a month before deciding it's not for you.

**Resistance band work.** Bands let you control load very precisely, which matters with arthritis. A light Theraband (the yellow ones, roughly one to three pounds of resistance) lets you work shoulder external rotation, hip abduction, and ankle mobility without the unforgiving fixed load of a dumbbell. Cando brand bands are widely used in clinical settings and usually run about $10 to $15 for a set. Clamshells (lying on your side with a band around your thighs, opening the top knee like a clamshell while keeping feet together) are one of the best hip strengtheners I know for people with hip and knee OA.

**[Gentle yoga, with caveats](/chair-yoga-for-seniors-beginners/).** Not all yoga is created equal, and some classes will wreck your joints if you're not careful. What you want is a chair yoga class or an arthritis-adapted yoga class, not a general beginner class where the instructor might not understand joint precautions. The app "Chair Yoga for Seniors" (roughly $3.99 on iOS and Android) is a reasonable starting point if in-person classes aren't accessible.

## What to Do on a Flare Day

Does any of this apply when your arthritis is actively flaring? When the joints are hot, swollen, and every movement feels like it's making things worse?

You scale way back, but you probably don't stop entirely. During a flare, the goal shifts from building strength or range of motion to preserving it. Gentle range-of-motion exercises done through a pain-free arc, nothing that reproduces or worsens the pain, can help prevent the joint from becoming completely stiff while protecting the inflamed tissue.

Here's what that looks like practically. If your knee is flaring: sit in a chair and gently swing your lower leg forward and back within the range where you feel no more than a 2 or 3 out of 10 on pain. Ten slow swings. That's it. You're maintaining the movement pattern without loading the joint. Same principle applies to fingers: gentle fist-making and opening in warm water, within a comfortable range, during a hand OA flare.

The old advice to ice inflamed joints isn't universally supported by current research. Some people find ice helpful, others find heat works better. For many of my clients with osteoarthritis (as opposed to rheumatoid), moist heat tends to win. A warm paraffin wax bath for the hands is something hand therapy clinics often offer, and many people swear by home paraffin baths (the Therabath Professional unit runs around $115, the WaxWel home version is about $45).

## The Pacing Piece Nobody Talks About Enough

Here's something I see go wrong constantly: someone has a relatively good day, feels encouraged, and does three times as much as they should. The next two days are miserable. They conclude that exercise makes their arthritis worse and stop.

This is called the "boom-bust" cycle and it's one of the most common barriers to consistent activity in people with chronic pain conditions. The fix sounds almost insultingly simple but it's actually hard to do: on good days, do less than you feel capable of. Keep a little in reserve. Build your baseline from average days, not your best days.

A practical rule I give people: if you feel significantly worse the day after an exercise session (not tired, but actually more painful and stiff than your usual baseline), you did too much. Scale back by 20 to 30% and hold that new level for at least a week before increasing. If you feel about the same or better the next day, you're in the right range.

## Before You Start

Please talk to your doctor or a physical therapist before beginning a new exercise program if your arthritis is severe, if you've had joint replacement surgery, or if you have other conditions that affect your exercise capacity like cardiovascular disease or osteoporosis. A physical therapist can assess your specific pattern of joint involvement and give you individualized guidance that no article can replicate. Many insurance plans, including Medicare Part B, cover physical therapy for arthritis-related limitations. It's worth checking your benefits.

Some days the goal is just to move your body enough to keep it from seizing up. That's a completely legitimate goal. Not every workout needs to be ambitious. Consistency at a modest level beats intensity followed by two days of recovery almost every time. You know your body better than any program does. Trust that, keep showing up, and adjust as you go.
