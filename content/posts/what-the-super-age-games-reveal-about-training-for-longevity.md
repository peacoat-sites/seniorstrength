On November 7, 2026, 960 people showed up to the Armory Track in New York City to compete in something that had never existed before. The inaugural Super Age Games, organized by longevity media company Super Age and developed with Dr. Eric Verdin, CEO of the Buck Institute for Research on Aging, and Dr. Andy Walshe, former Head of Performance at Red Bull, wasn't a race or a weightlifting meet. It was a competition built around the biomarkers scientists actually use to predict how long, and how well, people age: cardiovascular capacity, [grip strength](/grip-strength-is-now-a-vital-sign-here-is-what-to-do-about-it/), balance, agility, functional strength, endurance under load, working memory, and social connection. Athletes ranged in age from 29 to 80. Some were committed competitors. Others had never entered a fitness event in their lives.

That mix says something. The event landed in mainstream health media quickly, with NPR covering it on November 15 and Bloomberg following on November 17, both framing it as a broader cultural shift away from performance aesthetics and toward what researchers call healthspan. For adults over 60, the Super Age Games matter not because you need to register for the next one (though you could), but because the eight trials the event chose are a near-perfect mirror of what current science says actually predicts whether you'll [stay independent](/exercises-for-seniors-to-stay-independent/), mobile, and sharp into your 70s, 80s, and beyond.

The competition reframes the central question from "how fit are you?" to "how well is your body built to last?" Those aren't the same thing. A good 5K time doesn't tell you much about your [fall risk](/how-to-reduce-fall-risk-in-elderly-parents/). A big bench press number says almost nothing about your ability to get up off the floor. The Super Age Games made those gaps visible.

## Grip Strength Is Not Just a Gym Metric

Grip strength keeps showing up in longevity research because it's a reliable proxy for overall muscle quality and systemic health. Research consistently links grip strength to lower mortality risk in older adults, and grip strength was one of the eight trials the Super Age Games tested directly. That's a meaningful number, and grip strength was one of the eight trials the Super Age Games tested directly.

What this means practically: grip strength doesn't improve by squeezing stress balls. It improves through loaded pulling movements, things like deadlifts, farmer carries, rows, and hanging from a bar. The American College of Sports Medicine recommends that adults over 60 perform muscle-strengthening activities at least two days per week, targeting all major muscle groups. The pulling movements that build grip are also the ones that strengthen the posterior chain, which supports posture and spine stability. One exercise category, two problems addressed.

You can [see how your strength compares to others your age and sex](/strength-standards-by-age/) to get a clearer sense of where you stand before deciding where to focus.

## Functional Strength Looks Different From Traditional Strength

The Super Age Games didn't program conventional gym exercises. The functional strength trial was designed to test how well the body moves under load in patterns that reflect real life: getting up from the floor, carrying objects, stabilizing through asymmetrical movement. That distinction matters because a lot of traditional resistance training advice still treats reps and sets as the core variable, without much attention to movement pattern or lifting speed.

Research has found that faster, more explosive ("power") lifting outperforms slow, controlled lifting when it comes to everyday physical function in adults over 60. Slower lifting still led on muscle size. Here's what that tradeoff looks like:

| Lifting Approach | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Slow, controlled reps | Greater muscle hypertrophy (size) | Less transfer to functional, real-world tasks |
| Fast, power-based reps | Better performance on functional tests | Less muscle size gain |
| Combined approach | Addresses both outcomes | Requires programming awareness |

The National Institute on Aging recommends strength training that includes both seated and standing movements, with progression over time. Adding some intentional speed, lifting with controlled but deliberate power on the upward phase, is a simple adjustment that most adults over 60 aren't making. Talk with a physical therapist or certified trainer before making significant changes to your resistance training, especially if you have joint issues or a history of injury.

## Balance and Agility Are Being Neglected

Balance testing was part of the Super Age Games for a reason. Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death in adults over 65, according to the CDC, and balance is trainable. The problem is that most people over 60 who do exercise spend almost all of that time on cardiovascular work or resistance training, with almost no time on balance or agility.

The ACSM and the NIA both recommend balance training at least two to three times per week for older adults. Single-leg standing, heel-to-toe walking, standing on unstable surfaces, and reactive drills all build the neuromuscular coordination that keeps people upright when something unexpected happens, a curb they didn't see, an icy step, a surface that shifts.

Agility is the related piece. The Super Age Games included agility trials because reaction time and change-of-direction speed decline with age and rarely get trained. Lateral shuffles, cone drills, and simple footwork patterns can be built into almost any routine without specialized equipment. If you're unsure whether balance is already a concern, a [fall risk assessment](/fall-risk-assessment/) is a reasonable first step before adding new balance challenges.

## VO2 Max: The Number Most People Don't Know

Cardiovascular capacity, specifically VO2 max, the maximum rate at which the body can use oxygen during intense exercise, was included in the Super Age Games because it's one of the strongest individual predictors of longevity across the research literature. It declines roughly 10% per decade after age 30, but it responds well to training at any age.

Zone 2 cardio, steady-state aerobic work where you can hold a conversation but feel the effort, is the foundation. The CDC recommends at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity for older adults. Adding even one or two sessions of higher-intensity interval work, short bursts of harder effort followed by recovery, can push VO2 max higher than steady-state work alone. Cycling, swimming, brisk walking, and rowing are all good options with lower joint impact. The goal isn't to train like an athlete. It's to keep the cardiovascular system capable of handling the demands of a long life.

## What the Games Got Right That Most Fitness Culture Gets Wrong

The standard conversation about fitness for older adults still leans heavily on weight loss, general "staying active," and fall prevention framed as a fear-based afterthought. The Super Age Games took a different approach, treating longevity markers as performance targets worth training toward deliberately and competitively. Dr. Verdin's involvement from the Buck Institute meant the event was grounded in actual aging biology, not just wellness trends.

The inclusion of working memory and social capacity as competition categories is worth noting. These aren't add-ons. Cognitive decline and social isolation are both strongly linked to accelerated physical decline in older adults. Building fitness programs that include social components, group classes, training partners, community events, isn't just more enjoyable. It addresses a real biomarker.

The Super Age Games are almost certainly not a one-time event. The inaugural competition drew 960 athletes and landed in Bloomberg and NPR within two weeks. The framework it introduced, fitness measured by longevity biomarkers rather than performance aesthetics, is a genuinely useful lens for anyone over 60 thinking about how to train. Grip strength, balance, power, and cardiovascular fitness aren't separate concerns to juggle. They're a single picture of how well the body is aging, and all of them respond to the right kind of work.
