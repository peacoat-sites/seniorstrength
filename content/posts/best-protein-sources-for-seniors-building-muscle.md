You're at the gym, you've just finished your third set of seated leg presses, and the trainer hands you a protein shake made with some chalky powder that tastes like vanilla-flavored chalk dust. "You need at least 30 grams after every workout," he says. You nod, drink it, and wonder if this is really what it takes. Maybe. But what most people don't realize is that the protein question for adults over 60 is far more nuanced than a post-workout shake, and getting it wrong doesn't just slow your results. It can quietly work against the very muscle you're trying to build.

## Why Older Adults Need More Protein Than You've Been Told

Here's the number that surprises almost everyone: the standard Recommended Dietary Allowance for protein is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. That number was set to prevent deficiency, not to optimize muscle building. For a 70-year-old trying to get stronger, it's genuinely inadequate.

I've seen clients in their late 60s eating what they believed was "plenty of protein" because they'd hit the RDA. They were eating chicken salads, having a Greek yogurt at lunch, and calling it good. And they were still losing muscle. Why? After about age 60, your body becomes less efficient at converting dietary protein into muscle tissue. Researchers call this phenomenon "anodic resistance," or more commonly, anabolic resistance. Your muscles simply don't respond to protein signals the way they did at 40.

The practical fix: most sports nutrition researchers and geriatric dietitians now recommend somewhere between 1.2 and 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily for older adults who are actively strength training. For a 170-pound (77 kg) person, that's roughly 93 to 123 grams of protein per day. That's a meaningful target, and it matters which foods you use to hit it.

One more thing most people skip over: distribution matters as much as total intake. Spreading your protein across three to four meals, aiming for at least 25 to 40 grams per sitting, appears to maximize muscle protein synthesis better than loading it all into one or two meals. Your body can only use so much at once, and the excess doesn't get stored as muscle.

## The Protein Sources That Actually Deliver

Not all protein is created equal, and this is where the conversation usually gets oversimplified. What you're looking for is a combination of high leucine content, good digestibility, and overall amino acid completeness. Leucine is the key trigger for muscle protein synthesis. Think of it as the ignition switch. You need enough of it per meal (roughly 2 to 3 grams) to actually flip that switch.

**Eggs** deserve to be at the top of this list. A whole egg contains about 6 grams of high-quality protein, and the yolk carries half of it along with vitamins D, B12, and choline, which supports brain and nerve function. I've worked with clients who avoided yolks for decades based on old cholesterol fears. Current evidence is far more nuanced on dietary cholesterol for most healthy adults, but please talk to your doctor if you have specific cardiovascular concerns before changing your egg intake.

**Lean beef and bison** are worth revisiting even if you've been told to cut back on red meat. A 4-ounce serving of 90% lean ground beef delivers around 22 grams of protein along with creatine, zinc, and heme iron, all nutrients that support muscle function and energy. The creatine alone is meaningful: it's not just a supplement. It's a compound your muscles use for short-burst energy, and older adults tend to have lower natural levels of it.

**Greek yogurt and cottage cheese** are workhorses. Plain Greek yogurt (2% fat) has roughly 17 to 20 grams per cup. Cottage cheese, often underrated, provides about 25 grams per cup and is rich in casein protein, which digests slowly and may help preserve muscle overnight when eaten before bed. This is not speculation. There's a solid body of research on pre-sleep casein and muscle protein synthesis in older adults.

**Salmon and other fatty fish** give you protein and omega-3 fatty acids in one shot. Omega-3s, particularly EPA and DHA, have direct anti-inflammatory effects that support muscle recovery and may reduce the severity of anabolic resistance. A 3-ounce serving of salmon runs about 22 grams of protein. Aim for fatty fish two to three times per week if you can.

**Legumes and soy** matter, especially if you eat plant-forward. While most plant proteins are lower in leucine than animal sources, soy is the exception. Tofu, tempeh, and edamame are complete proteins with leucine levels that approach meat. A cup of cooked edamame contains about 17 grams of protein. For black beans or lentils, you're looking at 15 to 18 grams per cup, but combining them with a leucine-rich source like Greek yogurt or eggs in the same meal improves their muscle-building effectiveness.

## Building a Day of Protein Around Real Food

Let me walk you through what this actually looks like on a plate, because the numbers mean nothing if you can't fit them into your daily life.

**Morning (Target: 30-40g protein)**
Start with a 3-egg scramble with spinach and feta, plus a cup of Greek yogurt on the side with some berries. That combination gets you to around 35 to 38 grams before you've even left the kitchen.

**Midday (Target: 30-40g protein)**
A 4-ounce chicken breast over a large salad with chickpeas and a hard-boiled egg hits about 40 grams. Add olive oil and lemon, skip the croutons if you're watching calories, keep the chickpeas because the fiber helps.

**Afternoon (Optional, 15-20g protein)**
A cup of cottage cheese with pineapple or a small can of tuna on whole-grain crackers works well here, especially on days when dinner will be lighter.

**Dinner (Target: 30-35g protein)**
A 5-ounce salmon fillet with roasted vegetables and a side of lentil soup gets you to around 40 grams and checks your omega-3 box for the day.

**Before bed (Optional, 20-25g protein)**
This is where cottage cheese really earns its place. A cup before bed, maybe with a tablespoon of nut butter, provides that slow-release casein protein shown to support overnight muscle repair.

Total across this day: roughly 140 to 160 grams, which comfortably supports active muscle building for most adults in this range.

## Should You Use Protein Supplements?

I want to be honest here because there's a lot of money being made on supplements marketed specifically to older adults, and some of it is well-spent while some of it isn't.

Whey protein is genuinely effective. It's fast-digesting, high in leucine, and has decades of research behind it. If you're struggling to hit your protein targets through food alone, a quality whey isolate or concentrate can fill the gap without a lot of calories. It's not magic. It's just convenient food in powder form.

Collagen protein is where I pump the brakes a little. It's everywhere right now, marketed heavily to the 60-plus crowd for joints and skin. Collagen is a real and useful protein for connective tissue support, but it's not a complete protein and it's very low in leucine. If you're using it specifically for joint health under medical guidance, fine. But don't count it toward your muscle-building protein targets.

Creatine monohydrate is one of the most underused supplements in older adults and one of the most researched. A standard dose of 3 to 5 grams per day has been shown in multiple studies to improve strength gains and muscle mass in older adults when combined with resistance training. It's inexpensive, safe for most healthy people, and works synergistically with high-protein eating. Talk to your doctor before starting, particularly if you have kidney concerns.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you really build muscle after 70?

Yes, absolutely. The research on this is clear and encouraging. Adults in their 70s and even 80s show measurable increases in muscle mass and strength from resistance training combined with adequate protein. The process is slower than it was at 50, and you need to be smarter about recovery, but the biological capacity is there. I've seen clients in their mid-70s add meaningful functional strength within three to four months when they nail the training and the nutrition together.

### Is plant-based protein enough for muscle building in seniors?

It can be, but it requires more planning. Plant proteins generally have lower leucine content and lower digestibility than animal proteins. If you're fully plant-based, you'll likely need to aim for the higher end of the protein range (closer to 1.6g per kilogram), prioritize soy-based sources like tofu and tempeh, and consider a leucine supplement or plant-based protein powder that uses a blend of sources. It's doable. It just takes more attention.

### Does eating more protein harm your kidneys?

For adults with healthy kidney function, no. The concern about high protein and kidney damage applies primarily to people who already have diagnosed kidney disease. If you have chronic kidney disease or have been told to limit protein by your doctor, follow that guidance precisely. For everyone else, current evidence does not support the idea that a high-protein diet damages healthy kidneys. Still, when in doubt, check with your physician before making significant changes.

### How important is protein timing around workouts?

It matters, but it's not the emergency it's sometimes made out to be. The old idea of a 30-minute "anabolic window" has been largely revised. What research supports is getting a solid protein-rich meal within about two hours before or after training. Post-workout, aiming for 30 to 40 grams of protein that includes leucine is a reasonable target. Total daily intake distributed evenly across meals is probably more important overall than hitting an exact minute on the clock.

### What if I have a poor appetite and can't eat this much protein?

This is genuinely common in older adults and worth taking seriously, not brushing off. If appetite is a persistent issue, talk to your doctor because it can be a sign of something worth investigating. Practically speaking, liquid protein sources like Greek yogurt smoothies, protein shakes, and milk-based soups are often easier to get down than solid food. Eating smaller, more frequent meals can help. Adding calorie-dense, protein-rich foods like eggs, nut butter, and cheese to meals you already enjoy is more sustainable than trying to force down extra meals.

The gap between "eating enough" and "eating enough to actually build muscle after 60" is wider than most people expect, but it's absolutely closable with the right foods and a little structure. You don't need exotic supplements or perfect meals. You need consistent, adequate protein distributed across the day, paired with a resistance training program designed for where your body is right now. Work with a registered dietitian or sports medicine physician if you want a plan tailored to your specific health history. The effort is worth it. Stronger muscles mean better balance, fewer falls, more independence, and honestly, just feeling better in your own body. That's a return worth investing in.
